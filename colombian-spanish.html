A field guide to español colombiano

Hablar colombiano

¿Quiubo, parce?[ˈkju.βo ˈpaɾ.se]

Colombian Spanish is less one dialect than a family of them. The Caribbean coast sounds close to Cuba and Andalusia, while the Andean highlands keep some of the most conservative pronunciation in the Americas. Here are the sounds, the grammar quirks, the regions, and the words.

Overview

A national label for a diverse group of dialects

“Colombian Spanish” is more a geographic grouping than a linguistic one. The northern coast shares the innovations of Caribbean Spanish, such as softened or dropped final s. The highland varieties have historically changed much less.

The educated speech of Bogotá is widely admired across Latin America as clear and conservative. Two institutions anchor scholarship: the Academia Colombiana de la Lengua, founded in 1871 as the oldest Spanish-language academy after Spain’s own, and the Instituto Caro y Cuervo in Bogotá, which studies the language and literature of Colombia and Spanish America.

Some scholars even argue that El desierto prodigioso y prodigio del desierto, written in the 1600s in the New Kingdom of Granada by Pedro de Solís y Valenzuela, was the first modern novel of Spanish America.

Pronunciation

Seven sounds that give it away

/x/ → [h]

The Spanish “j” is a soft breathy h, not a scrape in the throat.

jamás [haˈmas]
Everywhere except Nariño, which keeps velar [x]
ll = y (yeísmo)

Most speakers merge “ll” and “y”. Older Bogotanos, Santander and the area around Pasto can still keep [ʎ]. In southern Antioquia and Norte de Santander, “ll” sounds like [ʒ], the “s” in English “measure”.

pollo [ˈpo.ʝo]
Exceptions: Santander, Pasto, older Bogotá
z = s (seseo)

No Spain-style “th”. cocer and coser sound identical. Yet many Andean speakers, especially in Medellín, use a tongue-tip [s̺] like northern Spain, a trait found nowhere else in the Americas.

abrazar = abrasar
Apical [s̺]: Antioquia, Pasto
b d g after consonants

After a consonant, these stay hard stops, where most other dialects soften them.

pardo [ˈpaɾdo], not [ˈpaɾðo]
Not in Nariño or most of the coast
b d g between vowels

Between vowels they go soft, and can disappear entirely.

Bogotá [bo.oˈta]
General
pʰ tʰ kʰ

Voiceless stops can carry a puff of air, almost like English p, t, k. Some Boyacá speakers also turn f and s into breathy sounds.

nosotros → nojotros
Parts of Cundinamarca and Boyacá
-s → [h], -n → [ŋ]

On the coasts, a syllable-final s becomes a breath and a final n moves to the back of the mouth.

costa [ˈkoh.ta], rosales [roˈsa.leh]
Caribbean, Pacific, Llanos

Vowels are the standard Spanish five. Words with vowels side by side, like teatro, tend to glide into one syllable. In formal speech, coastal speakers do this more than inland ones; in casual speech there is no difference.

Grammar · forms of address

Four ways to say “you”

Ustedeo
usted

Formal almost everywhere else, but in Bogotá, Boyacá, Santander and Tolima it is used between friends, spouses and family. Depending on tone, a sudden usted between a couple can also signal a scolding.

Voseo
vos

The informal “you” in the Paisa region and Valle del Cauca, especially Cali, with its own verb forms: vos tenés, vos sabés. It is non-standard, discouraged in schools, and declining. Bogotá used it until the 1800s.

Su merced
sumercé

Literally “your grace”. A warm, respectful address on the Cundiboyacense plateau. In Bogotá it is only marginal.

Archaic
vosotros

As in the rest of the Americas, Spain’s plural “you all” and its -áis/-éis endings survive only in church language. Everyone says ustedes.

Tú is still common, for example between a man and a woman of similar age in Cundinamarca, and it is gaining ground among young people in the Opita region. Coastal speakers also say subject pronouns out loud more often: about 34% of the time in Barranquilla, versus 22–26% in the highlands.

Grammar · diminutives

Making everything a little smaller

Colombians attach diminutive endings almost anywhere. After a stem ending in t, the ending becomes -ico/-ica instead of -ito/-ita, as in Cuba, Venezuela and Costa Rica.

gato→gaticoNoun with a t-stem: “kitty”.
corriendo→corrienditoEven a gerund: “running” becomes “scurrying”.
ahora→ahoritaAdverb. Careful: it often means “in a bit”, not “right now”.
junto a→juntico aA preposition: “right next to”.
el chocolate caliente→el chocolatico calienticoRedundant: noun and adjective both shrink. A cozy little cup of hot chocolate.
ahora mismo→ahoritica mismoEmphatic: two endings stacked. “Váyase ahoritica mismo” means get out this very second.
bueno→buenecitico“El carro está buenecitico”: the car is in tip-top shape.

Family talk turns roles around: spouses call each other mijo and mija (“my son”, “my daughter”), and parents call their children papito and mamita.

The national greeting
¿Quiubo?

A contraction of ¿Qué hubo?, literally “what was there?”, using the past tense of haber. It works like “what’s up?”. It started in the Paisa region, spread across the country, and is now heard abroad as the stereotypical Colombian hello. Also written kiubo or qui’iubo. In Cali it becomes ¿Q’hubo vé, bien o qué?

Dialect regions

Fourteen ways to sound Colombian

Linguists disagree on how to divide the map. Lipski counts four phonological zones, Canfield five, Flórez seven. The eleven areas most often listed cover the coasts, the Andes and the Llanos. This guide adds three more so the whole country is covered: Cauca’s highlands, the Amazon, and Vaupés–Guainía, where Spanish is mostly a second language. Those three are much less documented.

Costeño

Caribbean coast
Caribbean region: Barranquilla, Cartagena, Santa Marta, Montería, Valledupar, La Guajira, Urabá
  • Close to general Caribbean and Andalusian Spanish
  • Final s becomes [h]; final n becomes [ŋ]
  • Sub-accents: Samario (most articulated), Barranquillero, fast Cartagenero, crisp Sinú Valley (Montería)
costa [ˈkohta]-n [ŋ]

Isleño

Islands
San Andrés, Providencia, Santa Catalina
  • Caribbean Spanish mixed with English influence
  • Final r can be an English-like [ɹ], a trill, a tap or an l
  • Close to Nicaragua’s Caribbean coast; strong Afro-Caribbean influence
verso [ˈbeɹso]amor → amol

Chocoano · Pacífico

Pacific coast
Chocó and the whole Pacific coast: Buenaventura, Guapi, Timbiquí, Tumaco; Afro-Colombians inland in Cauca and Valle
  • African influence on intonation and rhythm
  • Final s aspirated or dropped; final n as [ŋ]
  • d becomes r in some words; final l and r merge
-s → ∅l = r

Rolo · Cachaco

Andean
Bogotá
  • Basis of standard Colombian Spanish
  • Strong ustedeo, even among intimates
  • Keeps final s and the d in -ado; older speakers keep ll ≠ y
usted-ado [aðo]

Cundiboyacense

Andean
Cundinamarca and Boyacá, the high plateau
  • Sumercé as a respectful “you”; usted for informal talk
  • s sometimes aspirated between vowels, but kept at word end
  • Aspirated p, t, k in some areas
sumercénojotros

Paisa

Andean
Antioquia, Caldas, Risaralda, Quindío; north Tolima and Valle
  • Coffee country, centered on Medellín
  • Apical [s̺], halfway between s and sh, as in northern Spain
  • Voseo, reinforced by writers like Tomás Carrasquilla and Fernando Vallejo
  • Home of the slang called parlache
[s̺]vospues

Valluno

Andean
Cauca River valley; capital Cali
  • Strong voseo
  • Tag question ¿sí o no? after statements
  • Local words: chuspa (plastic bag), chanclas (flip-flops), pachanguero (all-night partier)
vos¿sí o no?

Santandereano

Andean
Santander and Norte de Santander
  • Ustedeo in both formal and casual speech
  • Traditional speakers keep ll distinct from y
usted[ʎ]

Opita

Andean
Tolima and Huila, upper Magdalena valley; also much of Caquetá, settled from Huila
  • Slow tempo and distinctive intonation, linked to indigenous languages
  • Usted for everything; rural vusted
  • Voseo is rare; tú spreading among the young
vusted

Pastuso

Andean south
Andean Nariño around Pasto, and upper Putumayo (Sibundoy, Mocoa)
  • Velar [x] for j, unlike the rest of Colombia
  • Apical [s̺]; ll stays distinct from y
  • r can be a buzzing sibilant; b, d, g are tensed, not softened
[x][ʎ]

Caucano

Andean
Andean Cauca: Popayán and the central valley and highlands
  • Close to Valluno, with voseo in informal speech
  • Shades into Pastuso toward the border with Nariño
  • Shares the region with Nasa Yuwe (Páez), Namtrik (Misak) and Inga speakers; the Cauca coast speaks Pacific Spanish
vos

Amazónico

Amazon
Amazonas (Leticia), lower Putumayo and lower Caquetá
  • Closer to the Spanish of the Peruvian Amazon (Iquitos) than to inland Colombia
  • Borrowings from Indigenous languages and Portuguese; “portuñol” at the triple border with Brazil and Peru
  • Indigenous languages include Ticuna, Huitoto, Yagua, Cocama and Bora
  • Many settlers from the interior, so Andean accents are common in Leticia
portuñol

Vaupés–Guainía

Amazon
Vaupés (Mitú) and Guainía (Puerto Inírida)
  • For many people Spanish is a second language, learned after Tukano, Cubeo, Curripaco or others, and it carries their rhythm and sounds
  • In Vaupés, people traditionally marry outside their language group, so speaking three or four languages is normal; Tukano is the lingua franca
  • Settlers in the towns mostly bring Llanero and inland Andean accents
Spanish as L2Tukano

Llanero

Eastern plains
The Llanos east of the Cordillera Oriental: Meta, Casanare, Arauca, Vichada, Guaviare
  • Huge, sparsely populated area settled from the interior
  • Final s aspirated, and even s before vowels
-s [h]
Vocabulary

Words you’ll actually hear

Slang travels through telenovelas, comedy and films like Víctor Gaviria’s Rodrigo D: No Futuro. Much of it started in Medellín’s street slang and is now understood nationwide. Crude and offensive terms are hidden unless you switch them on.
